About CyberView

A rolling seven-day world map of publicly reported cyber events, for defenders. Free, non-commercial and open source.

How it is made

  • Collection: scripts read more than 130 government, vendor and news feeds in several languages, ask three independent news indexes (Google News, Bing News and GDELT) standing questions in 12 languages, and read primary databases: CISA's known-exploited list, ENISA's EU Vulnerability Database, NIST NVD, FIRST EPSS, Have I Been Pwned, cloud providers' status pages and leak-site tracking. No AI is involved in this step, and no single search engine decides what CyberView sees.
  • Corroboration: leads about the same thing are grouped into one story, and stories reported by more independent publishers are researched first. Leak-site listings are attackers' claims: they are never published as facts.
  • Research: scheduled AI jobs (Anthropic's Claude) turn leads into events. They work under written rules that are published in full: facts only from pages actually read, original government and vendor documents preferred, specific source links required, outages never called attacks without an official source, attacker claims marked unverified.
  • Second review: every new or changed event is re-read against its sources by a different AI agent from the one that wrote it, which passes it, corrects it or flags it. Each event shows whether that review has happened.
  • Checking: a verification job re-checks recent events against their sources, a watchdog reviews failures and reader reports, and every build validates every record. Corrections are logged publicly.
  • Severity follows exploitation: known-exploited flaws, public exploit code or an official "patch immediately" in widely used or internet-facing software are rated high.
  • Links and attribution on the globe: arcs connect events only where a source states the link (for example a breached supplier and a customer hit through it). Attribution arcs are drawn only when a government body or the investigating firm made the attribution, and name who made it; CyberView does not attribute attacks itself.
  • Workbench: the workbench ranks exploited flaws by an open score, compares the software versions you list against NIST NVD's affected ranges inside your browser, and shows check and fix steps that an AI job takes from the vendor's own advisory. Version data can lag or be incomplete: confirm against the vendor advisory before you rely on a "not affected".
  • Community feed: visitors' posts are reviewed before they appear, first by fixed rules and then by an AI moderator. They are the authors' own words, not CyberView's reporting, and are never used as a source for an event; a credible tip is passed to the research jobs to verify from real sources.
  • Tabletop exercises: each week an AI job turns one verified event into a practice exercise. The real facts carry their sources; the scenario for your organisation is invented for practice and says so.
  • Every routine and its last run are shown on the status page; the full history is in the archive and the public repository.

AI-assisted research can make mistakes. Read information, not advice before relying on an entry.

Sources and licences

  • Event summaries and structured data written for CyberView: you may reuse them with attribution ("CyberView") and a link to the event. Each event links to the articles and advisories it is based on; those remain the property of their publishers.
  • CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog: US government work, public domain.
  • EPSS scores: FIRST.org; see EPSS usage guidance.
  • CVSS data: NIST National Vulnerability Database. This product uses data from the NVD API but is not endorsed or certified by the NVD.
  • Internet disruption signals: IODA, Internet Intelligence Lab, Georgia Institute of Technology.
  • Disaster alerts: GDACS, a cooperation framework of the United Nations and the European Commission.
  • Leak-site claims: listings tracked by ransomware.live. Organisation names only; never stolen data.
  • Map: Natural Earth (public domain) via world-atlas; d3 (ISC licence) and topojson-client (ISC licence). Accounts use supabase-js (MIT licence).
  • Code: CyberView's own code is released under the MIT licence.

Corrections and removal requests

If an entry is wrong, use Report an error on the event, or open an issue in the repository. The verification job checks every report against the sources; when an original contradicts an entry, the entry is fixed and the correction is logged publicly with what changed.

If you are an organisation or person named in an entry and believe it is inaccurate, unfair, infringes copyright or breaches your privacy, contact us privately (below) with the event link and what is wrong. We review requests promptly, correct or remove content that is inaccurate or unlawful, and tell you the outcome. Attacker claims the named organisation has not confirmed are labelled as such and hidden by default; an organisation's denial is recorded when it is published.

Security disclosure

If you find a security problem in CyberView, please report it privately through GitHub's private vulnerability reporting, not in a public issue. Good-faith research that respects these rules is welcome: do not access other people's accounts or data, degrade the service, or use social engineering; give us reasonable time to fix the issue before disclosure. We do not pay bounties. Machine-readable details: security.txt.

Accessibility

We aim to meet WCAG 2.2 level AA. Event types are shown by both colour and shape using a colour-blind-safe palette, the map and lists work with a keyboard (press ? on the map for shortcuts), motion respects your reduced-motion setting, and every map event is also listed as text in the feed. Known limitation: the map and charts are visual summaries; the feed, archive and exports carry the same information as text. If something is hard to use, please tell us.
